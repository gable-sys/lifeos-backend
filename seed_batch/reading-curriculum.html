The Science Curriculum

The great universities of the 20th century — Chicago, Harvard, Oxford, Caltech — required all students regardless of major to engage with the primary texts of science. Not popular summaries, but the actual books that changed how humanity understood itself and the natural world. This is that curriculum, rebuilt from your library and supplemented with the classic texts that belong alongside it. The science track runs as a parallel strand — one science book always in progress alongside the main literary curriculum, roughly one per month.

Evolution, Natural History & Biology

Darwin is the anchor — primary text, not summary. Everything else in biology and ecology is a response to or extension of the argument Darwin made in 1859. The great field naturalists come next: Gilbert White inventing ecology before the word existed, Audubon and Peterson transforming how we see birds, E.O. Wilson synthesizing everything.

Charles Darwin — Tier I · Read in Q5 (Year Two)
The Origin of Species
Harvard, Chicago, and Oxford all required this as a primary text — not for biology students but for everyone. The argument is one of the great achievements of human prose reasoning. Read the Introduction, Chapters I, III, IV, and XIV at minimum. Chapter XIV's conclusion is the most quietly devastating ending in English scientific prose. "There is grandeur in this view of life..."
Richard Dawkins — Tier I · Read in Q5 alongside Darwin
The Selfish Gene
Published 1976, instantly canonical. Dawkins reformulates Darwinian evolution at the level of the gene rather than the organism and introduces the concept of the meme. The finest work of science writing of the late 20th century. Read Darwin first, then Dawkins — the conversation between them is the education.
E.O. Wilson — Tier II · Q6
On Human Nature · The Diversity of Life
Wilson's Pulitzer-winning synthesis of evolutionary biology and human behaviour. On Human Nature (1978) asks the hardest question: what does evolution imply about ethics, religion, and the meaning of human existence? The Diversity of Life is his great ecological work — the most eloquent account of what we are losing.
Ernst Mayr — Tier II · Q6
What Evolution Is
Mayr was the greatest evolutionary biologist of the 20th century. This late summary (2001) is the clearest account of what evolution actually is — written for the intelligent general reader after a lifetime of research. Read after Darwin and Dawkins to consolidate.
Stephen Jay Gould — Tier III · Dip throughout
The Panda's Thumb · Ever Since Darwin
Gould's collected essays — the best science essays written in English since Huxley. Pull these out whenever the longer books feel too dense. Each essay is complete and brilliant on its own.
Francis Crick — Tier III · Q6
What Mad Pursuit · The Double Helix (Watson)
The discovery of DNA as personal narrative. Watson's account is more dramatic and controversial; Crick's is more reflective. Read both for the inside view of how science actually works — ambition, competition, luck, and genuine insight.

Ecology, Natural History & the Literature of Place

The great tradition of natural history writing is as much literature as science. Gilbert White's Selborne (1789) invented ecology before Darwin. Thoreau's Walden invented environmental consciousness. Rachel Carson's Silent Spring changed the world. These books belong alongside Montaigne and Emerson — they are essays in the fullest sense.

Gilbert White — Tier I · Q3 (read alongside the ancients)
The Natural History of Selborne
Published 1789. The founding text of modern ecology — and one of the most beautiful books in English prose. A curate in a Hampshire village observing the natural world for thirty years with inexhaustible patience and curiosity. Virginia Woolf loved it. Keats read it. Darwin said it taught him how to observe. One letter per day alongside the primary reading.
Aldo Leopold — Tier I · Q4
A Sand County Almanac
The founding text of modern environmental ethics. Leopold invented the concept of "the land ethic" — the idea that human beings have moral obligations to the natural community, not just to other people. Required reading in ecology, philosophy, and environmental studies at every major university since its publication in 1949. The most important environmental book ever written.
Rachel Carson — Tier I · Q7
Silent Spring · The Sea Around Us
Silent Spring (1962) launched the modern environmental movement — one book that changed government policy, public consciousness, and the fate of species. The Sea Around Us won the National Book Award and is Carson at her most lyrical. Both required reading in the great university curricula after 1962.
Henry David Thoreau — Tier II · Q2
Walden
The foundational text of American environmental consciousness. Thoreau spent two years in a cabin by a pond and transformed the experience into the most influential piece of American prose after the founding documents. Read it slowly — one chapter per day alongside the Greeks. It is also philosophy: how to live, what is essential, what can be discarded.
Henry Beston — Tier II · Q3
The Outermost House
A year alone in a dune cottage on Cape Cod, 1928. The finest book of nature writing in the American tradition after Thoreau. Required at many New England universities through the 1970s. "The world today is sick to its thin blood for lack of elemental things."
Konrad Lorenz — Tier II · Q5
King Solomon's Ring · On Aggression
Lorenz won the Nobel Prize for his work on animal behaviour (ethology). King Solomon's Ring is the most charming science book of the 20th century — Lorenz writing about jackdaws, geese, and dogs with the combination of rigour and affection that only the greatest naturalists achieve. On Aggression is the harder, more controversial book: animal aggression and what it implies about human violence.
Bernd Heinrich — Tier II · Q6
Mind of the Raven · A Year in the Maine Woods
Heinrich is the heir to both Lorenz and Thoreau — a biologist who can write. Mind of the Raven is his masterpiece: fifteen years studying raven intelligence. A Year in the Maine Woods is his Walden. Both required in natural history courses at major universities from the 1990s onward.
John Muir — Tier III · Dip throughout
My First Summer in the Sierra · The Mountains of California
The founding texts of the American conservation movement. Muir's prose at its best is rapturous — the Sierra Nevada as religious experience, ecological observation as devotion. Dip in whenever you need air.
Robert Macfarlane — Tier III · Dip
The Wild Places · Mountains of the Mind
The finest living writer in the British natural history tradition. Macfarlane writes about landscape with the same precision and feeling that the best novelists bring to character. Pull these in when reading about ecology becomes too abstract.

Ornithology & the Art of Observation

Ornithology is the oldest and most democratic of the natural sciences — it requires only patience, attention, and a willingness to be outside. The great ornithology books are also masterpieces of observation and description. They teach you to see.

Roger Tory Peterson — Tier II · Begin Year One
A Field Guide to the Birds
Peterson's 1934 field guide invented the modern field guide and launched the mass popular interest in birds. Not just a reference book — a philosophy of attention. Keep it with you. The practice of identifying birds is a practice in the same careful observation that the best scientists and writers share. Use it in the field alongside anything else you are reading.
Peter Matthiessen — Tier I · Q6
The Wind Birds · The Snow Leopard
The Wind Birds is Matthiessen's account of the shorebirds of the Atlantic Coast — ornithology as meditation on impermanence and migration. The Snow Leopard is his masterpiece: a journey into the Himalaya in search of the snow leopard, and a journey into grief, Buddhism, and the nature of attention. It belongs simultaneously in the science strand and the Buddhist strand.
J.A. Baker — Tier I · Any quarter
The Peregrine
Published 1967. The greatest work of ornithology ever written — and one of the great works of English prose, full stop. Baker spent ten years following peregrine falcons across the Essex landscape and wrote a book of extraordinary intensity. Robert Macfarlane calls it "one of the finest nature books ever written." Werner Herzog considers it essential reading for filmmakers. Read it in autumn, outdoors when possible.
Audubon — Tier III · Encounter
The Birds of America — Selected Plates + Ornithological Biography
Audubon's great plates are visual documents of the natural world at a moment before industrial transformation. The Ornithological Biography — the text that accompanies the plates — is extraordinary nature writing. Encounter the plates in a library edition if possible.
Mark Cocker — Tier III
Birds Britannica · Crow Country
Cocker is the great historian of human relationships with birds. Birds Britannica is encyclopedic; Crow Country is a sustained meditation on rooks and landscape. Both teach you how to look.

Anthropology & Human Origins

Anthropology was required at every major university from the 1920s through the 1970s as the essential supplement to history — it asks what human beings are across cultures and time, rather than within a single tradition. The foundational texts remain essential.

James George Frazer — Tier I · Q4
The Golden Bough — Abridged Edition
The most influential work of comparative anthropology ever written. Frazer collected the myths, rituals, and magical beliefs of cultures across the world and showed their underlying structural similarities — the dying and rising god, the scapegoat, the sacred king. Eliot used it to write The Waste Land. Joyce used it to write Ulysses. Required at every major university 1900–1970. Read the abridged edition; it is still enormous and magnificent.
Claude Lévi-Strauss — Tier I · Q6
Tristes Tropiques
The most beautiful book ever written by an anthropologist. Lévi-Strauss's account of his fieldwork among the indigenous peoples of Brazil — and simultaneously a meditation on civilization, modernity, and the vanishing of the world. Sartre called it "the most beautiful book of the second half of the 20th century." Required at Paris, Cambridge, and Harvard from the 1950s.
Ruth Benedict — Tier II · Q5
Patterns of Culture
Benedict's 1934 masterwork arguing that cultures, like individuals, have distinct personalities and that no culture's values are universal. One of the most important books of the 20th century for breaking down cultural parochialism. Required at American universities from the 1930s through the 1970s.
Bronisław Malinowski — Tier II · Q5
Argonauts of the Western Pacific
The founding text of modern participant-observation fieldwork. Malinowski spent two years in the Trobriand Islands and produced a detailed account of the Kula exchange system that transformed anthropology. Required at Oxford and Cambridge from the 1920s.
E.E. Evans-Pritchard — Tier II · Q5
Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande
The classic of British social anthropology. Evans-Pritchard shows how the Azande system of belief in witchcraft is internally consistent and rational — a devastating challenge to Western assumptions of cognitive superiority. Required at Oxford from the 1930s.
Desmond Morris — Tier III · Q4
The Naked Ape
Morris's 1967 popular account of human beings as a species of primate — examining courting, rearing, fighting, feeding, and comfort-seeking through the lens of zoology. A bestseller that changed how educated people thought about human nature. Read alongside E.O. Wilson.
Jared Diamond — Tier II · Q7
Guns, Germs and Steel
Diamond's Pulitzer Prize–winning account of why some civilizations came to dominate others — not through racial or intellectual superiority but through geographical accidents of domesticable plants and animals, navigable rivers, and continental orientation. One of the most important books of popular science of the late 20th century.
Richard Wrangham — Tier III · Q7
Catching Fire: How Cooking Made Us Human
Wrangham's argument that cooking — not tool use or language — is the defining adaptation of the human species. Recent science that belongs with the classical anthropology.

Physics, Cosmology & Mathematics

Every educated person in the 20th century was expected to understand, at a conceptual level, what Newton achieved, what Einstein revised, and what quantum mechanics implies. Not to solve the equations — but to understand the argument. The books below make this possible without a mathematics degree.

Richard Feynman — Tier I · Q5
The Feynman Lectures on Physics — Vol. I (selected chapters) · Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!
Feynman's Lectures are the greatest work of physics pedagogy ever produced. Vol. I (mechanics, radiation, heat) — read Chapters 1–6 and you will understand what physics is trying to do and why it is beautiful. Surely You're Joking is the autobiography — the most enjoyable science book of the 20th century. Required at Caltech and imitated everywhere.
Albert Einstein — Tier II · Q5
Relativity: The Special and General Theory
Einstein's own popular exposition, written for the general reader. The argument that space and time are not absolute but relative to the observer — explained by the man who discovered it, in language that does not require calculus. Required at Chicago and Harvard as a primary text in their general education programmes.
George Gamow — Tier II · Q6
One Two Three... Infinity · Mr. Tompkins in Paperback
Gamow was the greatest populariser of physics in the mid-20th century. One Two Three... Infinity covers mathematics, physics, biology and cosmology with extraordinary range. Mr. Tompkins makes relativity and quantum mechanics tangible through a series of dreams. Both were standard texts in 20th century university general education.
Stephen Hawking — Tier II · Q7
A Brief History of Time
The best-selling science book of the late 20th century — and a genuine work of popular science that delivers what it promises. Black holes, the Big Bang, the arrow of time, the possibility of a unified theory. Read after Einstein and Feynman for the context.
G.H. Hardy — Tier I · Q3
A Mathematician's Apology
The most beautiful short book about mathematics ever written. Hardy — one of the greatest mathematicians of the 20th century — defends pure mathematics as an art form, not a utility. Written in 1940 when Hardy felt his mathematical powers fading. Required at Cambridge. Essential for understanding what mathematics is and why anyone would devote a life to it.
Euclid — Tier I · Q2
Elements — Books I, II, VI, XI
Chicago's Great Books program required Euclid as primary text alongside Homer and Plato. The reason: Euclid's Elements is the model for all rigorous reasoning — the demonstration that you can begin from simple axioms and derive, by pure logic, results of astonishing complexity and beauty. Books I and II (plane geometry) are the essential reading. Lincoln taught himself Euclid by candlelight to learn how to construct an argument.
Richard Courant & Herbert Robbins — Tier II · Q4
What Is Mathematics?
The classic introduction to mathematics for the intelligent non-specialist. Einstein recommended it. Required in many university general education programmes. Covers number theory, geometry, topology, functions, limits, and the calculus — not as techniques to memorise but as ideas to understand.
George Pólya — Tier II · Q3
How to Solve It
Pólya's 1945 guide to mathematical problem-solving — but really a guide to thinking. The heuristics Pólya describes (understand the problem, draw a diagram, find a related problem you can solve) apply equally to writing, to engineering, to diagnosis. Required at Stanford for decades.
Henri Poincaré — Tier III · Q5
Science and Hypothesis · The Value of Science
Poincaré was the last great mathematician to work across every field of mathematics simultaneously. These essays on the nature and philosophy of science were required reading at the grandes écoles and influenced Einstein directly. Brief, penetrating, essential.

History & Philosophy of Science

Chicago, Harvard, and Oxford all required a course in the history of science — not as history of technology but as intellectual history: how did human beings change their fundamental ideas about the natural world? Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions is the most cited academic book of the 20th century.

Thomas Kuhn — Tier I · Q6
The Structure of Scientific Revolutions
The most cited academic book of the 20th century. Kuhn introduced the concept of the paradigm shift — the idea that science does not progress smoothly but through revolutionary discontinuities in which one framework of understanding is overthrown by another. Read this and you will think differently about all knowledge, not just science. Required everywhere from 1962 onward.
Jacob Bronowski — Tier I · Q7
The Ascent of Man
Bronowski's 1973 companion to the BBC series is the finest popular history of science and human intellectual development ever written. From the first stone tools through Newton, Darwin, and Einstein, Bronowski traces the development of human knowledge as a continuous act of imagination. The final chapter — at Auschwitz — is one of the most devastating passages in 20th century prose.
Herbert Butterfield — Tier II · Q5
The Origins of Modern Science
Butterfield's 1949 account of the Scientific Revolution of the 16th and 17th centuries — Copernicus, Galileo, Newton. Required at Cambridge and many American universities through the 1970s. The argument: the Scientific Revolution was not a discovery of new facts but a change in the way people thought about facts.
Karl Popper — Tier III · Q6
The Logic of Scientific Discovery — Selected Chapters
Popper's criterion of falsifiability: a theory is scientific only if it can in principle be proved wrong. This is the sharpest philosophical account of what distinguishes science from non-science. Read Chapters 1–4 and the summary. Required at Oxford and LSE from the 1950s.
Alfred North Whitehead — Tier III · Q5
Science and the Modern World
Whitehead's 1925 account of how science shaped modern thought — and where it runs into limits. Harvard required it for decades. One of the most intellectually generous books ever written: Whitehead was a mathematician who could write about poetry.

Earth Sciences, Geology & Deep Time

Darwin read Lyell's Principles of Geology on the Beagle and it transformed his thinking. The concept of deep time — geological time, billions of years — is as intellectually revolutionary as the heliocentric universe or evolution. These books give you that concept.

John McPhee — Tier I · Q4
Annals of the Former World
McPhee's Pulitzer Prize–winning series on the geology of North America — the greatest work of science writing in the American tradition. McPhee follows geologists across the continent and explains deep time, plate tectonics, and the formation of the landscape with the narrative skill of a novelist and the rigour of a scientist. Required in geology courses and science writing programmes everywhere. Read Basin and Range as the entry point.
Charles Lyell — Tier III · Q3
Principles of Geology — Selected Chapters
Darwin read this on the Beagle. Lyell established that the Earth is vastly older than scripture suggested and that geological processes are uniform and ongoing — the same forces that shaped the past are shaping the present. This was the conceptual prerequisite for Darwinian evolution. Read the introduction and first five chapters.
Richard Fortey — Tier II · Q5
Life: A Natural History of the First Four Billion Years
Fortey's account of the history of life on Earth from the first cells to the present. A palaeontologist who writes with the range and pleasure of the best Victorian naturalists. Required in many natural history courses from the 1990s.

Chemistry, Medicine & the Sciences of the Body

Primo Levi — Tier I · Q7
The Periodic Table
Levi was a chemist and a survivor of Auschwitz. In The Periodic Table he organizes his autobiography around the elements — each chapter named for a chemical element that shaped or illuminated an episode in his life. The most beautiful book ever written about chemistry, and one of the most beautiful memoirs in any language. It belongs simultaneously in the science strand and the literature track.
Linus Pauling — Tier III · Q5
General Chemistry — Selected Chapters
Pauling's 1947 textbook was required at Caltech, MIT, and in science programmes at major universities for thirty years. The opening chapters on atomic structure and the nature of chemical bonds are essential conceptual reading — not problem sets, but the ideas.
Oliver Sacks — Tier II · Q6
The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat · An Anthropologist on Mars
Sacks was the greatest medical writer of the late 20th century. These case histories — neurological patients with extraordinary conditions — are simultaneously clinical documents and profound meditations on what it means to be a person. Required in medical humanities courses everywhere. Read as literature as much as science.

Ecology, Economics & the Natural World

You already have Wendell Berry, James Rebanks, and Paul Kingsnorth in your library. These belong in the science curriculum as well as the literary one — they are the tradition of thinking about human relationships with land that runs from Gilbert White and Thoreau through Leopold and Carson to the present.

Wendell Berry (you have it) — Tier I · Q4
The World-Ending Fire — Selected Essays
Berry is the great American writer on the relationship between human communities and the land. These essays are simultaneously ecological argument, political economy, and moral philosophy. Read "The Peace of Wild Things" first — his finest poem, which is also his central argument. Read alongside Aldo Leopold.
James Rebanks (you have it) — Tier II · Q3
English Pastoral: An Inheritance
Rebanks — a Lake District sheep farmer — has written the finest account of traditional farming and its destruction by industrial agriculture. Read alongside Gilbert White for the long view of the English landscape.
Robert MacFarlane — Tier II · Q4
The Old Ways · Underland
MacFarlane's two masterpieces. The Old Ways traces ancient footpaths and the knowledge embedded in landscape. Underland goes underground — caves, mines, the deep past beneath our feet. Together they complete a vision of the natural world that Gilbert White, Thoreau, and Leopold began.
Vaclav Smil (you have it) — Tier II · Q7
Energy and Civilization: A History
The most important book about the relationship between energy and human history. Smil argues that all of human history can be understood as the successive capture of new energy sources. Required reading in energy policy, economics, and environmental studies.

Buddhist & Contemplative Philosophy Strand — Both Years

Runs alongside the main curriculum. Buddhism and Greek philosophy are asking the same questions and arriving at different answers. Read them in parallel. Begin with Rahula — the scholarly entry point, not a popular introduction.

Begin Month 1
Walpola Rahula
What the Buddha Taught
A Theravada monk with a doctorate from the Sorbonne. Brief, clear, authoritative. The Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path, the nature of the self — without sentimentality. One week. Start here before anything else in the Buddhist strand.
The Dhammapada (Penguin Classics)
The Dhammapada — one chapter daily
423 verses. Read one chapter per day alongside your other reading throughout both years. The opening verse: "Mind is the forerunner of all actions." Read alongside Job and notice two ancient traditions asking the same question.
Tier II — Year One into Year Two
Bhikkhu Bodhi (trans.)
In the Buddha's Words
The definitive Pali Canon anthology in English. Your scholarly foundation after Rahula.
Shantideva
The Way of the Bodhisattva — Chapter VI (Patience)
Read Chapter VI alongside Marcus Aurelius. Both writing about responding to anger without being destroyed by it. The Buddhist and Stoic answers differ; the question is identical.
Nagarjuna (trans. Jay Garfield)
Mulamadhyamakakarika — Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way
Graduate-level Buddhist metaphysics — you already have it. Read after Rahula and Bodhi. Nagarjuna: nothing exists independently; everything arises in dependence on other things. Read alongside Plato's Parmenides — two of the greatest philosophical minds thinking about the same problem from opposite starting points.
Surendranath Dasgupta (you have it)
A History of Indian Philosophy Vol. I
Use as scholarly context for the Buddhist primary texts throughout.
Peter Matthiessen
The Snow Leopard
A journey into the Himalaya in search of the snow leopard — and simultaneously a Buddhist meditation on grief, impermanence, and attention. Belongs in both the science strand (ornithology, natural history) and the Buddhist strand.

Tier I — Deep Read
Plato (Adam's Cambridge ed. — you have both volumes)

The most important book in Western thought — not because it is right but because everything subsequent is arguing with it. Take six weeks. Mark everything you disagree with. The education is in the disagreement.

Robert Graves

The great 5th century mystical theologian whose writings shaped medieval Christian thought more than almost any other figure. Dante placed him in Paradiso. Bloom's Note

Shakespeare is the center of the Western literary canon. Not Homer, not Dante — Shakespeare. He invented the human as we understand it: the self that overhears itself, that changes through self-reflection. Read at least one play aloud, even alone — these are scripts, not poems; they require the breath.

Shakespeare (Complete Works)

Cervantes
Don Quixote — Part II
The first metafiction — the characters know they are in a novel. This is Borges and Nabokov, written in 1615. The ending is among the most heartbreaking in all literature.
Milton (Fowler's Routledge ed.)
Paradise Lost
Books I, II, IV, IX essential. Satan is the first modern hero — ambitious, self-created, magnificent, and wrong. Blake thought Milton was unconsciously on Satan's side. He may have been right.

The Origin of Species

Written 1790, before the Terror. More frightening read after the Terror — because Burke predicted it.
James Boswell (you have it)

The most outrageous autobiography in any language. Goethe said it was the fullest portrait of a man he had ever read.

Read immediately after Darwin. Dawkins reformulates Darwinian evolution at the level of the gene and introduces the concept of the meme. The finest work of science writing of the late 20th century. Together Darwin and Dawkins constitute the two essential texts in the life sciences.

Tier I — Deep Read
Dostoevsky (Pevear & Volokhonsky) — The Complete Academic Sequence

Crime and Punishment
Done. Raskolnikov's theory, its failure, and the long descent into confession.

The Idiot
Next. Dostoevsky's attempt to portray a perfectly good man — Prince Myshkin — and show how the world destroys him. You need the full weight of C&P and Brothers K to feel what Myshkin's goodness costs. The most heartbreaking of the four great novels. Begin after Monte Cristo (May 2026).

Demons
After The Idiot. The most political of the four — a revolutionary cell in a provincial town, based on a real murder. The darkest of the four and the one that reads most like prophecy about the 20th century.
Tolstoy

Flaubert (Steegmuller trans.)
Madame Bovary · Sentimental Education
Madame Bovary: the first truly modern novel. Sentimental Education: the novel about failing to live your life, in which nothing happens for 600 pages and the reader feels the weight of every wasted year.
Thomas Kuhn
The Structure of Scientific Revolutions
The most cited academic book of the 20th century. Kuhn introduced the concept of the paradigm shift — science does not progress smoothly but through revolutionary discontinuities. Read this and you will think differently about all knowledge. Required everywhere from 1962. Read it alongside Dostoevsky — both are about the violent overthrow of a prevailing worldview.

Zola
Germinal · Nana
Germinal: the supreme novel of labour and class. Nana: the flesh and its consequences.
Herman Melville
Moby-Dick

George Eliot
Middlemarch
The greatest English novel. Dorothea Brooke is Eliot's argument about what Victorian England did to women of extraordinary capacity.
Stendhal
